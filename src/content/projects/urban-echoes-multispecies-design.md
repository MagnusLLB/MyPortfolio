Most technology is designed for humans. This project, done with a classmate for a 10 ECTS IT
product development course at Aarhus University, asked what happens if interaction design also
takes other species into account. We chose a species that almost everyone in a city has an
opinion about: the common pigeon.

We worked as a research-through-design project, in a probing way: instead of following a fixed
plan, each exercise and discussion opened up a new perspective that fed the next. We started out
wanting to design something for wild pigeons. Over the course of the project we drifted from
animal-computer interaction towards human-centred design, but with wild animals as an important
part of the experience. The result is an early prototype of an audio-AR app called **Urban
Echoes**, which we see mostly as a vehicle for what we learned.

## The Process

### Why pigeons?

We were fascinated by how the relationship between humans and pigeons has changed. People have
kept pigeons for thousands of years. They lived in pigeon towers in the ancient world, where
their droppings (guano) were used as fertiliser, and later they carried messages across empires
and through the First World War. Once better fertilisers and postal services came along, the
pigeon lost its role. In the 1940s and 50s pigeons were accused of spreading disease without any
proof, and in 1966 the phrase "rats with wings" was born. Today most city dwellers think of them
as pests.

### A meta-design for pigeons

Our first exercise was a meta-design for the pigeon's new place in society, on the thinking that
making pigeons useful again might make people welcome them back. The pigeon tower sits at the
centre: towers that produce guano for citizens' gardens, paid for by donations or small fees.
Around it we collected ideas, questions and research on pigeons.

### Pigeon-computer interaction

Designers have a huge body of research on human bodies and senses to build on, and we wanted
something similar for pigeons. We read up on their biology and found a wide field of view, a
strong sense of smell, a magnetic sense, an excellent sense of direction, and even partial
success at the mirror test. We mapped these senses and used them to look for design
opportunities.

![A Miro board mapping a pigeon's senses (vision, smell, sound, touch, taste) with sticky notes of ideas for each](/images/projects/urban-echoes-multispecies-design/process-2-pigeon-senses.png)

_A map of the pigeon's senses and ideas for designing with them._

Then we tried to turn human artefacts, such as a boom box and a backpack, into pigeon artefacts.

The exercise showed us how we were thinking. Much of what we came up with was worn, which wild
animals have not evolved to do, or was meant to be gripped with the beak or claws. It also made
us realise that our designs didn't let the pigeons take part in the design in any real way, or
fit into the natural systems already in place. A better direction might have been to make objects
look like natural things the animal already knows.

### Out in Aarhus

We needed a more personal feel for pigeons, so we surveyed the pigeons of Aarhus. They tended to
retreat into hard-to-reach parts of buildings when people came too close, and there were far
more of them in dense city areas than in the forest at Moesgaard. We also noticed how much
dropping there was under the places where they gathered.

### Rats with wings

Reading Colin Jerolmack's paper on how pigeons became rats, we saw how public opinion of pigeons
changed over 150 years. He argues that it comes from a wish to keep nature controlled and tidy
inside cities. Our reading was that the "problem" began when pigeons were given a role in society
and then dropped. Once we no longer needed them, they were left to fend for themselves, which
people now see as an invasion.

That changed our design challenge. If we gave pigeons a new place that depended on some fragile
role, we would only repeat history. We wanted them to be seen more as companions or neighbours
than as employees, resources or pests. So the aim became to create positive interest in pigeons
by giving people "pigeon literacy", a better ability to read what a pigeon is up to, much like
knowing that a dog wants the ball thrown.

We also looked at **boundary objects**, things that different communities can use in different
ways. Buildings, which pigeons use as artificial cliffs, turned out to be a negative one for
people, because of claw marks and droppings. Bird baths were a positive one: the birds enjoy them
and people find the birds make the baths nicer. That idea, that the presence of animals makes an
experience better, carried through to the final prototype.

### Asking the experts

The biggest turning point was talking to people who know pigeons. A pigeon owner told us how hard
even an imprinted pigeon finds technology, and that pigeons show feelings in ways people don't
recognise, such as an affectionate peck or a rhythmic head bob. That supported our idea that
humans lack pigeon literacy.

We also talked to the head of the local branch of the Danish Ornithological Society (DOF). He
thought the hardest part of any design for wild pigeons would be getting their attention and
teaching them behaviours. Food could motivate them, but they already have plenty, and we didn't
want to make them more dependent on people anyway. He also helped us see why the society's
sighting maps show lots of pigeons at the harbour: partly the food, but also because bird
watchers go there for the rare birds and report what they see. That planted the idea of a more
precise map of the city's animals.

These conversations were the final blow to designing for wild pigeons within this project. It
needed a deep understanding of the animal and skills from outside our field, which we couldn't
build in the time we had.

### Back to people, with animals in mind

We went back to what interaction design is good at, designing for people, but our time with the
pigeons had changed what we considered part of an experience. We narrowed it down to three ideas:
cameras on rental bikes around the city, a similar device you could borrow at the library or
find in a park, and using soundscapes as an indicator of biodiversity.

The second idea was a pair of binoculars that can switch to a view from stationary cameras,
showing something you normally can't see, such as the inside of a birdhouse or the leaves of a
tree.

![A pencil sketch showing a before and after view of a tree, with an x-ray view of what is inside it](/images/projects/urban-echoes-multispecies-design/process-7-binoculars-sketch.jpg)

_Binoculars that switch to a camera showing a view you couldn't otherwise see._

We also sketched a game for the binoculars, "Pigeon Snap", where you earn points for spotting
and photographing animals. We dropped it so that people would be motivated by their own interest
rather than by points.

![A five-step pencil storyboard of a game where a person looks through binoculars, sees an overlay and earns points for photographing pigeons](/images/projects/urban-echoes-multispecies-design/process-8-pigeon-snap-storyboard.jpg)

We developed the third idea, the soundscape, into Urban Echoes.

### The concept: Urban Echoes

Urban Echoes highlights the animals that live in a city through local soundscapes. When someone
spots an animal, by hearing or seeing it, they record a sighting and the app stores the position,
the time and the animal. Another user who walks within a radius of that point (100 metres in the
prototype) hears the soundtrack. More sightings mean a more intense, more frequent soundtrack,
and where nothing has been seen there is silence. In that way it leaves digital traces of the
animals that show what lives in a neighbourhood, or that nothing does.

There are two roles, the **information gatherer** and the **sound seeker**. You can add a
sighting with a recording, such as a seagull's call, or with a photo of the animal, and the plan
is to use generative AI to turn that into a sound bite.

The purpose is twofold. One part is to build a map of the city's animals that researchers, city
planners and nature enthusiasts can use, inspired by citizen-science projects such as My
Nature Watch. The other is to give people an experience of the traces of nature around them,
which we also see as a quiet form of climate action, since the data helps the people looking
after nature.

A few design choices follow from this:

- **An app, not a special object.** Phones are everywhere, so it costs people nothing extra in
  money or in fiddly interactions.
- **Eyes off the phone.** We wanted people to look at their surroundings, so we decided against
  something like a time slider, which would pull their attention to the screen.
- **A personal experience, not a speaker.** Playing sounds out loud in public spaces could disturb
  the wildlife, so the sound is meant for individuals. Headphones come at a cost, though, because
  they cut people off from the real sounds around them, so keeping the connection to the real
  soundscape is something to balance.

### The prototype

The prototype is an Android app with hard-coded points. Each point has a sound file and a radius
of 100 metres. When you walk into a point, its sound starts quietly and grows louder as you get
nearer to the middle, reaching full volume there, which helps you sense where the animal was
recorded. Where points overlap, you hear both. The sounds are pre-recorded rather than
AI-generated, and the screen shows debug information such as GPS coordinates. For now you can
only listen. A full version would let people upload their own sightings, which is what would
build the map. We haven't evaluated the prototype, so we can't say how well it works.

## What I Learned

- **Wicked problems take time.** Designing for several species is one, and a 10 ECTS project
  can't solve it. Working on it did make me think about the responsibility designers have, not only
  towards people but towards nature and the environments we have built.
- **Stay with the problem.** Our supervisor encouraged us to, and it was hard. It often felt like
  hitting a wall, but a new perspective or a bit of new knowledge usually made it passable.
- **Talk to the experts.** What got us out of the biggest dead ends wasn't more reading or
  arguing but asking people who know the domain, in our case the people who know pigeons.
- **The exercises are the visible part.** Much of our time went on discussion between them, and
  the exercises were what we used when we felt stuck.
- **Traditional HCI methods don't carry over.** You can't ask a wild animal for clarification, and
  techniques such as ethnographic fieldwork don't work in the usual way.

These are our own experiences rather than proven findings. There is plenty left to explore: letting
animals interact with the app so that it becomes a triangle of animals, people and technology,
representing sounds outside the range of human hearing through something like vibration, studying
similar apps such as Merlin Bird ID, and finding the right balance between real and augmented
sound.
